\abstract{

Adverse childhood experiences are strongly associated with lifelong health,
social, and economic outcomes, and increasing evidence suggests that their
effects may propagate across generations. While empirical studies document the
prevalence, transmission, and consequences of trauma, there is currently no
unified framework linking these individual-level mechanisms to population-level
dynamics. Here we combine a minimal analytical model with a data-informed
simulation model to study the long-term evolution of trauma prevalence. The
analytical model yields a closed-form threshold determining when trauma becomes
self-sustaining, while the simulation model incorporates heterogeneous exposure
levels and empirically grounded transmission patterns. The models show how
demographic processes, intergenerational transmission, and recovery interact to
produce stable endemic trauma levels or long-run decline, and establish the
conditions under which interventions can eliminate endemic prevalence.

}

\section{Introduction}

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) are widely recognised as major
determinants of lifelong health, social, and economic outcomes. The original
ACE study demonstrated strong associations between early adversity and a range
of adult disease risks \cite{felitti1998relationship}. Subsequent work has
shown that exposure to multiple forms of adversity is common across populations
\cite{joshi2021prevalence}. Meta-analytic evidence further indicates that
cumulative childhood adversity is strongly associated with both physical and
mental health outcomes throughout the life course
\cite{hughes2017effect}, with recent estimates suggesting that childhood
maltreatment alone may account for a substantial fraction of global mental
health burden \cite{grummitt2024burden}.

Trauma may also propagate across generations. Intergenerational transmission of
adversity has been documented across a range of social and developmental
contexts \cite{madigan2019intergenerational,schickedanz2021intergenerational},
and proposed mechanisms include behavioural pathways, socioeconomic
constraints, and biological processes such as epigenetic regulation
\cite{yehuda2016intergenerational}. Developmental research further emphasises
that early adversity can shape long-term stress responses and behavioural
trajectories \cite{shonkoff2012lifelong}, while resilience and recovery
processes can mitigate these effects under favourable conditions
\cite{masten2014ordinary,skolnick2023association}.

Exposure to harsh or unpredictable environments has been linked to accelerated
life-history strategies and earlier reproductive timing \cite{ellis2009fundamental},
and empirical studies have found associations between childhood adversity and
fertility patterns \cite{williams2019adverse}. Population prevalence is therefore
shaped by the interplay between transmission, demographic processes, and recovery.

No theoretical framework yet links these mechanisms to the long-term population
dynamics of trauma. Existing studies focus either on individual outcomes or on
statistical correlations across cohorts. The first group documents associations
between ACE exposure and adult health risk \cite{felitti1998relationship},
synthesises dose--response relationships \cite{hughes2017effect}, and
characterises developmental consequences of early toxic stress
\cite{shonkoff2012lifelong}. The second reports ACE prevalence estimates
\cite{joshi2021prevalence} and intergenerational transmission rates
\cite{madigan2019intergenerational}.
Neither approach addresses how trauma prevalence evolves over long time horizons
or responds to structural interventions. Population modelling has proven useful
for linking demographic processes to long-run outcomes \cite{caswell2001matrix},
but has not been applied to trauma dynamics.

We develop a minimal analytical model that captures the essential mechanisms
required for trauma to persist or decline in a population. This model isolates
the roles of differential reproductive outcomes, intergenerational transmission,
and healing, and yields explicit threshold conditions for self-sustaining trauma
prevalence. We complement this with a data-informed simulation model
incorporating heterogeneous exposure levels and empirically grounded
transmission probabilities, which we use to explore how realistic distributions
of adversity shape long-run population outcomes.

Throughout this work \emph{adversity state} refers to an individual's level of
cumulative ACE exposure, not to any single event. Within a generation,
individuals move between lower and higher adversity states through exposure or
healing; across generations, adversity propagates via behavioural, socioeconomic,
and biological pathways. These mechanisms are illustrated in
Figure~\ref{fig:framework-overview}.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Analytical model}

Individuals are assumed to be either traumatised or not, so the
population state is \(x=(x_T,x_S)\), where \(x_T\) denotes the proportion of
traumatised individuals and \(x_S=1-x_T\) the remainder.

Let \(f_T\) and \(f_S\) denote the fitness of the respective subpopulations.
Consistent with evidence linking adversity to accelerated life-history
strategies and elevated fertility, we set \(f_T=\alpha f_S\) with \(\alpha>1\)
\cite{ellis2009fundamental,williams2019adverse}.

We allow both the introduction of new trauma and the possibility of recovery
\cite{yehuda2016intergenerational,skolnick2023association}, modelled through
two parameters:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \(p\): probability that a non-traumatised individual becomes
    traumatised,
    \item \(q\): probability that a traumatised individual recovers.
\end{itemize}

Together, \(\alpha\), \(p\), and \(q\) determine the balance between
transmission, recovery, and differential reproduction, and hence whether trauma
prevalence grows, declines, or stabilises over time (Figure~\ref{fig:trauma-dynamics}A).

The population evolves according to the discrete replicator--mutation dynamic
\cite{imhof2005evolutionary,nowak2006evolutionary},
\[
x_i'=\sum_{j\in\{T,S\}}\frac{x_j f_j Q_{ji}}{\bar f},
\qquad i\in\{T,S\},
\]
with mutation matrix
\[
Q=
\begin{pmatrix}
1-q & q \\
p   & 1-p
\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The resulting update rule for the traumatised proportion is
\[
x_T'
=\frac{x_T f_T(1-q)+x_S f_S p}{\bar f}.
\]

To assess persistence, consider the growth factor
\(\kappa_T=x_T'/x_T\).
Substituting \(f_T=\alpha f_S\) and \(x_S=1-x_T\) yields
\[
\kappa_T
=\frac{1}{x_T}
\frac{x_T\bigl(\alpha(1-q)-p\bigr)+p}{x_T(\alpha-1)+1}.
\]
Values \(\kappa_T>1\) imply growth of the traumatised subpopulation,
while \(\kappa_T<1\) imply decline, reflecting the standard 
growth criterion used in evolutionary dynamics
\cite{nowak2006evolutionary,hofbauer1998evolutionary}.

Interior equilibria satisfy \(x_T'=x_T\), leading to the quadratic

\[
(\alpha-1)x_T^2+(1+p-\alpha+\alpha q)x_T-p=0,
\]

Because the constant term equals \(-p<0\) while evaluation at
\(x_T=1\) gives \(\alpha q\ge0\), continuity guarantees a root in
\((0,1)\) whenever \(p,q>0\).
This root defines the endemic level \(x_T^*\), showing that any
persistent introduction of trauma produces a stable long-run
prevalence (Figure~\ref{fig:trauma-dynamics}A).

When no new trauma is introduced (\(p=0\)), persistence depends on invasion
from rarity. Taking the limit \(x_T\to0\) gives
\[
\lim_{x_T\to0}\kappa_T=\alpha(1-q),
\]
so trauma spreads from low prevalence precisely when
\[
\alpha(1-q)>1
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
q<1-\frac{1}{\alpha}.
\]
This condition acts as a demographic threshold separating extinction from
endemic persistence, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:trauma-dynamics}C.

\medskip

Long-run trauma prevalence is therefore governed by the balance between
transmission, recovery, and demographic reinforcement. A sufficient reproductive
advantage can sustain trauma across generations even without continual exposure,
and recovery must cross a critical threshold to eliminate it. The system behaves,
in this respect, like an infectious agent with a demographic amplification
mechanism.

Figure~\ref{fig:trauma-dynamics}B--C summarises the equilibrium structure.
The next section embeds these mechanisms in a data-informed simulation that
adds demographic stochasticity, age structure, and empirically grounded
parameterisation.

\section{Individual-based simulation model}

To explore how stochasticity, finite-population effects, and demographic
heterogeneity modify the analytical predictions, we built an individual-based
simulation of the same mechanisms.

\subsection*{Individual state and population dynamics}

Each individual is characterised by \((s, a, k, \bar{k})\) denoting sex
\(s \in \{\text{M}, \text{F}\}\), integer age \(a \geq 0\), current ACE count
\(k \in \{0, 1, \dots, 8\}\), and the count accumulated by the end of childhood
\(\bar{k} \geq k\), which is fixed from age 18 onwards.  The population at time
\(t\) is the multiset
\(\mathcal{P}_t = \{(s_i, a_i, k_i, \bar{k}_i)\}_{i=1}^{N_t}\), where \(N_t\) is
the (stochastic) population size.  The model evolves in discrete yearly steps;
in each step three processes act sequentially on every individual:
reproduction, mortality, and within-life ACE adjustment.  Reproduction precedes
mortality, so an individual who dies during year \(t\) may still give birth in
that year.

\subsection*{Mortality}

Each individual dies in year \(t\) with probability \(\mu(s, a)\),
drawn from age- and sex-specific life-table values based on UN World Population
Prospects data \cite{un_wpp_mortality}.  Mortality is assumed independent of
ACE count, so demographic selection operates exclusively through fertility.

\subsection*{Fertility and ACE transmission}

Reproduction is restricted to females and is resolved before mortality, so a
female who dies during the year may still give birth in it.  The baseline
probability of a birth in a given year for a female of age \(a\) is
\(b_0(a)\), taken from UN
age-specific fertility schedules \cite{un_wpp_fertility}.  Two empirically
motivated mechanisms modify this baseline for ACE-exposed individuals.

\subsubsection*{Tempo effect (earlier reproductive timing)}
Consistent with evidence that adversity is associated with accelerated
life-history strategies and earlier age at first birth
\cite{williams2019adverse}, the fertility schedule is shifted earlier in
proportion to ACE exposure.

We distinguish two counts.  Write \(k\) for an individual's current ACE count,
which healing reduces, and \(\bar{k}\) for the count accumulated by the end of
childhood.  Since healing acts only on adults, \(\bar{k}\) is the total an
individual was ever exposed to and is fixed from age 18 onwards, while
\(k \leq \bar{k}\) is what remains after any recovery.  The tempo effect reads
\(\bar{k}\) and the quantum effect reads \(k\).

A female then evaluates her birth probability at an effective age
\[
    \tilde{a} = a_0 + (a - a_0)\,\pi(\bar{k}),
    \qquad
    \pi(\bar{k}) = 1 + \gamma \bar{k},
\]
where \(a_0 = 15\) is the youngest age in the fertility schedule and
\(\pi(\bar{k})\) is the \emph{life-history pace}.  The birth probability is
scaled by the same factor,
\(b(a,k) \propto \pi(\bar{k})\, b_0(\tilde{a})\), so that the schedule is
compressed rather than translated and completed fertility is preserved.

We use \(\bar{k}\) rather than \(k\) here because life-history pace is
calibrated during development \cite{ellis2009fundamental} and is not reset by
later recovery.  Reading the current count instead would allow an individual
who had aged out of her compressed fertile window to re-enter it by healing,
so that recovery manufactured fertility rather than retiming it.
We set \(\gamma = 0.314\), calibrated so that a single ACE lowers the mean age
at childbearing by three years, matching the estimate of Williams and Cheadle
\cite{williams2019adverse}.
We rescale rather than translate because a rigid shift of an age-bounded
schedule moves the fertile window below \(a_0\), and for large \(k\) pushes it
off the schedule entirely, which destroys completed fertility and produces
implausible ages at birth.  Under rescaling the shift in mean age at
childbearing saturates on its own, from three years at \(\bar{k}=1\) to nine
years at \(\bar{k}=8\), so no cap is required.
This implements a purely timing adjustment: the expected number of births over
a full reproductive span is unchanged to within 0.2\% across \(\bar{k}\).  Net
reproduction, which weights each birth by the probability of surviving to that
age, rises slightly (2.1\% at \(\bar{k}=8\)) because earlier childbearing is
less discounted by mortality.  This is a demographic consequence of the timing
shift rather than an additional fertility mechanism, and its size depends on
the mortality schedule.
The resulting schedules are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:model-specification}A,
and their consequences for the timing and the total of reproduction in
Figure~\ref{fig:model-specification}B.

\subsubsection*{Quantum effect (differential reproductive intensity)}
Conditional on effective age \(\tilde{a}\), the birth probability of a female
with \(k \geq 1\) ACEs is scaled by a multiplicative factor \(\alpha > 1\):
\[
    b(a, k) =
    \frac{\pi(\bar{k})\, b_0(\tilde{a})}{\bar{\alpha}}
    \times
    \begin{cases}
        1      & \text{if } k = 0, \\
        \alpha & \text{if } k \geq 1,
    \end{cases}
\]
where \(\bar{\alpha}\) is the population mean multiplier, weighted by the
fertility each individual would have at \(\alpha = 1\),
\[
    \bar{\alpha}
    = \frac{\sum_i \pi(\bar{k}_i)\, b_0(\tilde{a}_i)\,
            \alpha^{\mathbf{1}[k_i \geq 1]}}
           {\sum_i \pi(\bar{k}_i)\, b_0(\tilde{a}_i)},
\]
summed over females.  Dividing by \(\bar{\alpha}\) holds the total expected
number of births at its \(\alpha = 1\) value, so that \(\alpha\) redistributes
reproduction towards exposed individuals rather than raising it for the
population as a whole, while the ratio between the two groups remains exactly
\(\alpha\) (Figure~\ref{fig:model-specification}C).  This is the normalisation
the replicator dynamic of Section~\ref{sec:methods} performs when it divides by
mean fitness \(\bar f\); without it, raising \(\alpha\) would raise the
population growth rate as well as the relative standing of the exposed, and the
two models would not share a parameter.
Under a coarse-grained binary classification (ACE-free versus ACE-exposed),
this corresponds directly to the relative fitness parameter of the analytical
model, in which \(f_T = \alpha f_S\).  The simulation therefore embeds the
replicator structure of the analytical model within an age-structured,
stochastic demographic framework.

\subsubsection*{Intergenerational ACE transmission}
The ACE count of each newborn is drawn from an empirically estimated
conditional distribution \(\Pr(k_{\text{child}} \mid k_{\text{mother}})\), based
on reported intergenerational ACE correlations
\cite{schickedanz2021intergenerational}.
Denoting the four maternal exposure categories as \(\{0, 1, 2\text{--}3,
\geq 4\}\), the conditional probabilities are summarised in
Table~\ref{tab:intergenerational}.  Offspring ACE counts in the range
\(\{2, 3\}\) and \(\{4, \dots, 8\}\) are distributed uniformly within their
respective groups.  This mechanism allows adversity to propagate across
generations in the absence of ongoing environmental shocks, providing the
simulation analogue of the transmission parameter \(p\) in the analytical model.

\subsection*{Within-life ACE dynamics}

ACE counts may change within a lifetime through two complementary processes
that correspond directly to the analytical parameters \(p\) and \(q\).

\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Trauma exposure} (\(p\)): a child aged \(a < 18\) acquires one
    additional ACE with probability \(p\) per year, subject to the ceiling
    \(k \leq 8\).  This represents environmental and social transmission of
    adversity during the developmental window.
    \item \emph{Healing} (\(q\)): an adult aged \(a \geq 18\) reduces their ACE
    count by one with probability \(q\) per year, subject to the floor \(k \geq 0\).
    This represents recovery processes including therapeutic intervention,
    social support, and self-forgiveness
    \cite{skolnick2023association,trauma_recovery_processes}.
\end{itemize}

The net annual change in ACE count for individual \(i\) is therefore
\[
    \Delta k_i =
    \begin{cases}
        +1 & \text{if } a_i < 18 \text{ and } U_i < p, \\
        -1 & \text{if } a_i \geq 18 \text{ and } U_i < q, \\
        0  & \text{otherwise,}
    \end{cases}
\]
where \(U_i \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)\) is drawn independently each year.
\(p\) and \(q\) play the same roles in the simulation as in the analytical
model: \(p\) governs the rate of acquiring adversity and \(q\) the recovery rate.
The two processes act on disjoint phases of life, so a child cannot heal
while still accumulating adversity (Figure~\ref{fig:model-specification}E).

\subsection*{Initial conditions}

Simulations are initialised from a population pyramid based on UK Office for
National Statistics census data \cite{ons_population_pyramid}, from which ages
and sexes are sampled according to empirical proportions.  Initial ACE counts
are drawn from sex-stratified prevalence distributions reported in the Canadian
Longitudinal Study on Aging \cite{joshi2021prevalence}.

\subsection*{Simulation protocol}

We swept the three parameters that the analytical model shares with the
simulation.  The healing probability \(q\) takes 61 values from \(0.00\) to
\(0.60\) in steps of \(0.01\); the reproductive multiplier \(\alpha\) takes ten
values from \(1.05\) to \(1.50\) in steps of \(0.05\); and the background
exposure probability \(p\) takes the eleven values from \(0.00\) to \(0.10\) in
steps of \(0.01\), together with \(0.20\) as a saturated case retained for the
supplementary material.  This gives 7{,}320 parameter combinations.

Each combination was simulated for 50 independent replicates of 200 years,
starting from 10{,}000 individuals, for a total of 366{,}000 runs.  Newborns are
assigned male with probability \(0.51\), and an individual is counted as
traumatised when \(k \geq 1\).  Summary quantities are averaged over the years
from 100 onwards, discarding the first 100 years as a warm-up during which the
population relaxes from the initial age structure and ACE distribution to its
long-run state; Figure~\ref{fig:simulation-outcomes}A--B shows that
this is comfortably longer than the transient.

Every run is seeded explicitly, so the sweep reproduces exactly.  Each
parameter combination draws from its own block of 50 consecutive seeds, and the
blocks are disjoint, so replicates are independent both within and across
combinations.  A single generator is shared between the construction of the
initial population and the run itself, so each replicate consumes one
continuous random stream.  Between-replicate variation is shown throughout as
an inter-quartile range or a standard deviation.

\subsection*{Relationship to the analytical model}

The simulation preserves the core dynamical structure: \(\alpha\), \(p\), and
\(q\) enter with the same meanings as in the replicator--mutation model. It
replaces the mean-field assumption with explicit demographic stochasticity and
age structure, and the binary ACE state with a discrete count on
\(\{0, \dots, 8\}\).  In the limit of large population size and binary
aggregation of the ACE count, the simulation dynamics converge to the analytical
predictions; departures reflect stochasticity and heterogeneity.

To identify the boundary between high- and low-adversity parameter regimes in
the simulation output we applied \(k\)-means clustering (\(k=2\)) to the
log-transformed mean ACE surface; full details of this procedure are given in
Supplementary Information~\ref{si:kmeans}.

Figure~\ref{fig:population-dynamics} illustrates the baseline demographic
behaviour of the simulation across six parameter combinations
(\(q \in \{0.00, 0.01, 0.05\}\), \(\alpha \in \{1.05, 1.35\}\), no background
exposure): population size and mean age settle within a few decades across all
combinations (panels A--B), and the growth-corrected survivorship curves fall
on the theoretical life-table predictions irrespective of ACE parameters,
confirming that mortality is ACE-independent (panel C).  The correction is
needed because a population snapshot yields period rather than cohort
survivorship; the caption describes it.  Population age pyramids at 50-year intervals
(panels D--H) show the transition from the initial UK age structure to the
long-run steady-state shape for a representative parameter set
(\(q = 0.05\), \(\alpha = 1.35\), no background exposure).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

We have combined a minimal analytical model of trauma transmission with a
data-informed individual-based simulation of the same mechanisms.

Our central result is a closed-form threshold, \(q < 1 - 1/\alpha\), separating
parameter regimes in which trauma necessarily declines from those in which it
persists endemically.  When both a positive background exposure and a positive
recovery rate are present (\(p, q > 0\)), the quadratic fixed-point equation
admits an interior root, so any sustained environmental adversity produces a
stable long-run prevalence rather than eventual elimination.  We show in
Supplementary Information~\ref{si:stability} that this root is unique and
globally stable, and that the threshold is an exchange of stability between the
trauma-free and endemic states rather than merely a sign condition.

The threshold condition \(\alpha(1-q) > 1\) is structurally analogous to the
basic reproduction number \(R_0\) in infectious-disease epidemiology
\cite{hofbauer1998evolutionary}, with the product \(\alpha(1-q)\) playing the
role of \(R_0\).  Unlike standard SIS and SIR frameworks, this system contains a
demographic amplification term: adversity is not merely transmitted but is
reinforced by a reproductive advantage that standard epidemic models lack.

\subsection*{What the simulation adds}

The two models do not share a time step, and comparing their thresholds
requires the mapping we derive in Supplementary
Information~\ref{si:timescales}: the analytical parameters act once per
generation on a binary state, while the simulated ones act once per year on a
graded count and are confined to disjoint phases of life.

On that common footing the two thresholds differ in both level and slope, and
the way they differ is itself a result.  Converted to a per-generation
probability, the fitted boundary without background exposure,
\(q^*(\alpha) = 0.021\alpha + 0.042\) (\(R^2 = 0.99\)), runs from \(0.47\) at
\(\alpha = 1.05\) to \(0.52\) at \(\alpha = 1.50\).  The analytical threshold
over the same range runs from \(0.05\) to \(0.33\).  The simulated boundary
therefore sits above the analytical one throughout, by a factor of \(1.6\) at
\(\alpha = 1.5\) rising to an order of magnitude as \(\alpha \to 1\), and it is
markedly flatter: it moves by \(10\%\) across the swept range of \(\alpha\)
where the analytical threshold moves by a factor of seven.

The two discrepancies have different sources.  The level reflects structure the
analytical model omits.  Healing reaches an individual only during the years
between adulthood and reproduction, and it removes one adverse experience at a
time rather than clearing the state outright, so suppressing adversity in an
age-structured population is harder than a mean-field account implies.  The
flatness reflects something else.  An individual has \(D = 9.7\) annual healing
draws before reproducing, and at the rates in question that fixes the
per-generation recovery probability near one half almost regardless of
\(\alpha\).  What governs whether adversity persists is how many opportunities
for recovery a life affords, rather than the size of the reproductive
advantage.  Neither discrepancy is a failure to reproduce the analytical
result: together they measure what age structure and a graded state contribute,
neither of which a mean-field model can represent.  We reach the same
conclusion below from the endemic levels themselves, by a different route.

Background exposure raises the boundary substantially, to
\(q^*(\alpha) = 0.032\alpha + 0.156\) at \(p = 0.05\) (\(R^2 = 0.98\)).  The
positive intercepts in both fits imply a minimum healing requirement that
persists even as \(\alpha \to 1\), so a population with no reproductive
differential at all still requires sustained recovery to suppress adversity.

\subsection*{Transmission dominates the demographic channel}

An observation we did not anticipate is how little of the long-run outcome the
reproductive differential accounts for.  Across the full swept range of
\(\alpha\), from \(1.05\) to \(1.50\), the mean ACE count rises by between
1.1\% and 6.0\% depending on the healing rate and on background exposure
(Figure~\ref{fig:simulation-outcomes}C--D), while varying \(q\) over a
comparable range moves it by about 45\%.  The endemic level is set
principally by the intergenerational transmission distribution
\(\Pr(k' \mid k)\), which is fixed by data, and only secondarily by the
demographic mechanism the analytical model is built around.  We report this
because it bears directly on where intervention effort is best directed, and
because it qualifies the analogy above: the demographic amplification term is
real and it moves the threshold, but in a realistically parameterised
population it is not what determines how much adversity there is.

Because fertility is normalised by the population mean multiplier
(Section~\ref{sec:methods}), neither \(\alpha\) nor \(q\) has a demographic
footprint: the growth rate is \(-0.0007\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}\) across every
parameter combination we simulate (Figure~\ref{fig:population-dynamics}A).
This is by construction, and it is the property that lets \(\alpha\) mean the
same thing in both models.  It also means the simulation makes no claim about
how adversity affects population size, only about how it is distributed.

\subsection*{A floor that healing cannot reach}

Because exposure acts only on children and healing only on adults, sustained
childhood exposure sets a floor on population adversity that no adult recovery
rate can penetrate.  At \(\alpha = 1.35\) and a healing probability of
\(0.60\), an implausibly high rate, the mean ACE count still settles at
\(0.91\) when \(p = 0.10\) and at \(1.37\) when \(p = 0.20\).  Above an
exposure rate of roughly \(0.1\) per childhood year there is therefore no
healing rate that brings the population below the threshold at all.

This has a direct reading.  Adult recovery and childhood exposure are not two
interchangeable levers on the same quantity.  Where ongoing exposure is high
enough, reducing it is not one option among two but a precondition, and
therapeutic provision alone cannot substitute for it however well resourced.
The analytical model cannot express this, because it applies both processes to
the whole population every generation; it is a consequence of age structure and
so appears only in the simulation.

\subsection*{Intervention}

To drive the system below endemic persistence, \(q\) must exceed
\(1 - 1/\alpha\), and any intervention that reduces \(\alpha\) simultaneously
lowers the required healing rate.  Interventions combining socioeconomic
support with expanded therapeutic access therefore act on both factors in
\(\alpha(1-q)\).  The fitted relationship \(q^*(\alpha) = m\alpha + c\) makes
the requirement explicit for a given population, and the positive intercept
sets a floor below which no reduction in \(\alpha\) alone suffices.

We emphasise that \(\alpha > 1\) is a descriptive demographic rate.  It
reflects observed associations between early adversity and accelerated
life-history strategies \cite{ellis2009fundamental,williams2019adverse} and
carries no implication that trauma is adaptive, nor any normative content
whatever.  The model is an accounting of population-level flows and says
nothing about individuals.  Its intervention implications run entirely through
reducing exposure and supporting recovery.

\subsection*{Limitations}

Both models simplify adversity: the analytical model uses a binary state, the
simulation a discrete count on \(\{0,\dots,8\}\), and neither captures
continuous severity or multi-domain ACE categorisations.  The uniform
allocation of probability mass within the reported \(2\text{--}3\) and
\(\geq 4\) transmission categories is an assumption of convenience, and the
ceiling at eight binds at high exposure rates, so results at
\(p \geq 0.1\) are partly a saturation effect.

Mortality is ACE-independent, though evidence links high ACE burden to
premature mortality.  Whether including it would raise or lower the persistence
threshold depends on when the excess mortality falls relative to the
reproductive window, and we do not attempt to resolve that here.
Transmission is conditioned on maternal exposure only, and no spatial or
network structure is included, so the clustering of adversity within families
and neighbourhoods is absent; since clustering reduces effective mixing, we
expect a structured model to require more healing rather than less.

Finally, the model draws its parameters from four different populations, as set
out above.  Consistent single-population calibration, and the cross-cultural
variation it would expose, remain unexplored.

\subsection*{Conclusion}

We have shown that a minimal model of intergenerational adversity admits an
explicit persistence threshold, that an age-structured simulation reproduces
its dependence on the reproductive differential while requiring substantially
more recovery than the mean-field account predicts, and that sustained
childhood exposure imposes a floor which adult recovery cannot reach.  The
framework gives population-level adversity science a formal structure it has so
far lacked.  Whether real populations sit above or below the threshold is an
empirical question, and one we hope to address with cohort data in future work.

\subsection{Stability of the endemic equilibrium}
\label{si:stability}

The main text locates the fixed points of the update map but does not address
their stability.  We record that here.  Write the map as
\[
    g(x)=\frac{Ax+p}{Bx+1},
    \qquad
    A=\alpha(1-q)-p,
    \qquad
    B=\alpha-1 .
\]

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:stability}
Let \(\alpha>1\) and \(p,q\in(0,1)\) with \(p+q<1\).  Then \(g\) has a unique
fixed point \(x^*\) in \((0,1)\), and every orbit starting in \([0,1]\)
converges to it.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since \(Bx+1\ge 1\) on \([0,1]\), the map has no pole there.  Evaluating at the
endpoints gives \(g(0)=p\) and \(g(1)=\alpha(1-q)/\alpha=1-q\), both in
\((0,1)\), so \(g\) maps \([0,1]\) into itself.

Differentiating,
\[
    g'(x)=\frac{A-Bp}{(Bx+1)^2},
    \qquad
    A-Bp=\alpha(1-q)-p-(\alpha-1)p=\alpha\bigl(1-q-p\bigr),
\]
which is positive when \(p+q<1\), so \(g\) is strictly increasing.

Because \(Bx+1>0\), the net change \(\Delta(x)=g(x)-x\) has the opposite sign to
the quadratic \((\alpha-1)x^2+(1+p-\alpha+\alpha q)x-p\).  That quadratic has
leading coefficient \(\alpha-1>0\) and constant term \(-p<0\), so the product of
its roots is \(-p/(\alpha-1)<0\) and exactly one root is positive.  Since
\(\Delta(0)=p>0\) and \(\Delta(1)=-q<0\), that root lies in \((0,1)\) and is the
unique fixed point there.

Uniqueness and monotonicity together give \(\Delta>0\) on \([0,x^*)\) and
\(\Delta<0\) on \((x^*,1]\).  For \(x_0<x^*\) the orbit is therefore increasing,
and because \(g\) is increasing it remains below \(x^*\); a bounded monotone
orbit converges, and its limit is a fixed point, hence \(x^*\).  The case
\(x_0>x^*\) is symmetric.  The boundary cases \(x_0\in\{0,1\}\) are immediate,
since \(g\) maps them into the interior.
\end{proof}

The condition \(p+q<1\) is not restrictive: both parameters are annual
probabilities well below one half throughout.  We verified the proposition
numerically on 4{,}000 randomly drawn parameter sets, in every case recovering a
unique interior fixed point with \(|g'(x^*)|<1\) and convergence from both
endpoints.

Setting \(p=0\) recovers the threshold of the main text as a stability
statement.  The trauma-free state \(x=0\) is then a fixed point with
\(g'(0)=\alpha(1-q)\), so it is asymptotically stable precisely when
\(\alpha(1-q)<1\).  At \(\alpha(1-q)=1\) it loses stability in a transcritical
bifurcation, at which the interior equilibrium
\(x^*=1-\alpha q/(\alpha-1)\) enters \((0,1)\).  The threshold
\(q<1-\alpha^{-1}\) is thus an exchange of stability between the trauma-free and
endemic states, not merely a sign condition.

\subsection{Relating the two time scales}
\label{si:timescales}

The parameters \(p\) and \(q\) do not carry the same units in the two models,
and the two thresholds cannot be compared until they do.  In the analytical
model each acts once per generation on a binary state.  In the simulation each
acts once per year on a count in \(\{0,\dots,8\}\), and each is restricted to a
phase of life: exposure to childhood, healing to adulthood.

Three separate differences follow.  First, the time step: the generation time
implied by the fitted schedules, measured as the mean age at childbearing for an
unexposed individual, is \(T=27.7\) years.  Second, the phase restriction: an
individual is subject to healing only between age 18 and reproduction, a window
of \(D=T-18=9.7\) years, and to exposure only over the 18 years of childhood.
Third, the state space: one healing event decrements the ACE count by one, so an
individual carrying \(k\) ACEs requires \(k\) such events to become
untraumatised, whereas one recovery event in the analytical model clears the
state outright.

Writing \(q_{\mathrm{yr}}\) and \(p_{\mathrm{yr}}\) for the annual rates, the
number of healing events before reproduction is
\(\mathrm{Binomial}(D, q_{\mathrm{yr}})\), so an individual with \(k\) ACEs
recovers within one generation with probability
\[
    q_{\mathrm{gen}}(k)=\Pr\bigl[\mathrm{Binomial}(D,q_{\mathrm{yr}})\ge k\bigr],
    \qquad
    q_{\mathrm{gen}}(1)=1-(1-q_{\mathrm{yr}})^{D},
\]
and correspondingly
\(p_{\mathrm{gen}}=1-(1-p_{\mathrm{yr}})^{18}\) for acquiring at least one ACE
during childhood.  The two-state analytical parameters correspond to the
\(k=1\) case.  For orientation, \(q_{\mathrm{yr}}=0.03\) maps to
\(q_{\mathrm{gen}}=0.255\) and \(p_{\mathrm{yr}}=0.02\) to
\(p_{\mathrm{gen}}=0.305\): annual rates of a few percent are per-generation
probabilities of a few tens of percent, an order of magnitude apart.

We emphasise that this mapping is exact only for the \(k=1\) case in a
stationary population.  It neglects the dependence of \(T\) on \(k\) through the
tempo effect, and it treats the ACE count as though recovery from \(k>1\) were
the only additional obstacle.  Any residual discrepancy between the two
thresholds after this transformation is therefore not a units artefact: it
measures the contribution of age structure and of the graded adversity state,
neither of which the mean-field model can represent.

\subsection{\(k\)-means threshold procedure}
\label{si:kmeans}

To identify the boundary between parameter regimes that sustain high and low
levels of adversity, we applied \(k\)-means clustering with \(k = 2\)
\cite{lloyd1982least} to the log-transformed mean ACE surface.
Let \(\bar{k}(q, \alpha)\) denote the mean ACE count averaged across all years
and simulation replicates at healing probability \(q\) and reproductive
multiplier \(\alpha\), and let
\(\ell(q, \alpha) = \log\bigl(1 + \bar{k}(q, \alpha)\bigr)\)
be its \(\log(1 + \cdot)\) transform, applied to compress the dynamic range
of the surface and to handle zero values stably.
\(k\)-means partitions the observed values of \(\ell\) into two clusters by
iteratively assigning each value to its nearest cluster centre and updating
centres to cluster means until convergence. We initialise with \(k\)-means++
\cite{arthur2007kmeans}, keep the best of ten restarts, and fix the random seed so
that the threshold is reproducible. The scalar threshold \(\ell^*\) is the midpoint
between the two converged cluster centres, and requires no user-specified cutoff.
We compute \(\ell^*\) once, on the values of \(\ell\) pooled over \(p = 0\) and
\(p = 0.05\), and apply the same \(\ell^*\) at every value of \(p\), so that the
threshold heal rates reported in Figure~\ref{fig:simulation-outcomes} and
Figure~\ref{fig:threshold-vs-p} are on one scale and can be compared directly.
The threshold defines a level set
\(\{(q, \alpha) : \ell(q, \alpha) = \ell^*\}\)
in the original parameter space, which we locate for each value of \(\alpha\)
by linear interpolation along the \(q\)-axis to obtain crossing points
\(q^*(\alpha)\).
Although the threshold is identified in log-space, the resulting boundary is
expressed in the untransformed coordinates \((q, \alpha)\), and is well
described by a linear relationship
\[
    q^*(\alpha) = m\alpha + c,
\]
recovered by ordinary least squares regression on the interpolated crossing
points (fits shown in panel titles; \(R^2 > 0.95\) in all cases).
The boundary is therefore close to linear in \(\alpha\) over the swept range,
with a positive intercept.  It does not follow the analytical threshold
\(q = 1 - \alpha^{-1}\) derived in Section~\ref{sec:methods}: once both are
expressed per generation (Supplementary Information~\ref{si:timescales}) the
simulated boundary is higher and markedly flatter, as we discuss in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.
Figure~\ref{fig:threshold-vs-p} shows the resulting threshold contours for
every value of \(p\) in the data whose surface crosses \(\ell^*\).
